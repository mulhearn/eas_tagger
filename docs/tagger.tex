\section{Differential Intensity of Extensive Air Showers}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figs/fluxes.pdf}
    \caption{Incident differential intensity ($dJ/dE$), as presented
      by the Cosmic Ray Database (CRDB).}
    \label{fig:cridi}
\end{figure}

\noindent
The differential\footnote{Take care that some sources use J for this
quantity, but we are following the more explicit notation of e.g. the
CRDB} intensity $dJ/dE$ of cosmic rays incident at the top of the
atmosphere (TOA) is defined as
$$dJ/dE = \frac{dN}{dE \, dA \, dt \, d\Omega}$$
where $E$ is the energy of the incident primary and $t$ is time.
The quantities $d\Omega$ and $dA$ are the infinitesimal solid angle,
relative to normal, and the infinitesimal area, both {\bf at the TOA}.
The differential intensity has units:
$$[dJ/dE] = \left[ \frac{1}{\rm energy \; m^2 \; sr \; s} \right]$$
where sr is a dimensionless reminder to integrate over solid angle.  The incident
differential intensity at the top of the atmosphere is independent of
direction and follows a power law:
\begin{equation}
\label{eqn:djde}  
\frac{dJ}{dE} = \frac{\alpha}{\epsilon} \left( \frac{E}{\epsilon} \right)^{-\gamma}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is a reference energy and $\gamma ~\sim 3$. The energy-integrated intensity above some energy $E$ is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eqn:je}
J(E) \; \equiv \;
\int_{E}^{\infty} \frac{dJ}{dE} \, dE \; = \;
\frac{\alpha}{\gamma-1} \left( \frac{E}{\epsilon} \right)^{1-\gamma}
\; = \;
\frac{E}{\gamma-1} \, \frac{dJ}{dE}
\end{equation}
which we can write as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eqn:eje}
E \; \frac{dJ}{dE} = (\gamma - 1) \; J(E) 
\end{equation}
which shows that the $y$-axis in Fig.~\ref{fig:cridi} is proportional
to the integrated rate above, as indicated by the labeled gray bands.
For example, there are:
$$24 \cdot 60 \cdot 60 \sim 10^5$$
seconds per day, so the once-per-day band appears at:
$$E \; \frac{dJ}{dE} = 10^{-5} \; \frac{1}{\rm m^2 \; sr \; s}$$
Just take care, when interpreting these bands as integrated rates,
that the factor of $\gamma - 1$ has been neglected, so the actual
rates are about one half that indicated.

Equation~\ref{eqn:je} also implies that:
\begin{equation}
\label{eqn:eje-power}
E \; \frac{dJ}{dE} = \alpha \, \left( \frac{E}{\epsilon} \right)^{1-\gamma}
\end{equation}
which allows us to read off the parameters $\alpha$ and $\gamma$
easily from Fig.~\ref{fig:cridi}.  First note that:
$$E \; \left. \frac{dJ}{dE} \right|_\epsilon = \alpha$$
so we can read off $\alpha$ as the value of the $y$-axis for energy $E=\epsilon$.
For example, taking $\epsilon=10^9~\rm GeV$ we have
$$\alpha=10^{-11} \; \frac{1}{\rm m^2 \; sr \; s}$$
Next, taking the log of both sides of Equation~\ref{eqn:eje-power} yields:
\begin{equation}
 \label{eqn:eje-slope}
 \ln \left( E \frac{dJ}{dE} \right) = \ln(\alpha) + (1-\gamma) \ln \left( \frac{E}{\epsilon} \right)
\end{equation}
which shows that the power law is consistent with the linear
relationship revealed in the log-log plot of Fig.~\ref{fig:cridi}.
The $y$-axis drops by two orders of magnitude for every one order of
magnitude increase of the $x$-axis, from which we read off:
$$ 1 - \gamma = -2 \implies \gamma = 3$$
To recap, the quantity
$$E \frac{dJ}{dE},$$
has several useful properties when plotted versus energy in a log-log scale, as in Fig.~\ref{fig:cridi}:
\begin{itemize}
\item The $y$-axis is proportional to integrated rate above $E$.
\item The log-log plot is linear for a power-law.
\item The parameter $\alpha$ can be estimated from the $y$-axis value for $E=\epsilon$.
\item The parameter $\gamma$ can be estimated from the slope.
\end{itemize}
The extent to which Fig.~\ref{fig:cridi} is linear is a visual
indication of the accuracy of the power law.  We further improve the
accuracy of the power-law model by selecting parameters near the
region of interest.

\section{Dimensionless Log-Energy Units}

For computational work across many orders of magnitude in energy it is
helpful to define and work with the dimensionless log-energy variable:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eqn:defu}
  u \equiv \ln \left( \frac{E}{\epsilon}\right)
  \iff E = \epsilon \, e^u
\end{equation}
from which it follows that:
$$
du = \frac{dE}{E}
$$
and
\begin{equation}
 \label{eqn:djdu-edjde}
 \frac{dJ}{du} = E \frac{dJ}{dE}
\end{equation}
The power law of Equation~\ref{eqn:djde} becomes:
\begin{equation}
\label{eqn:djdu}
\frac{dJ}{du} = \alpha \exp((1-\gamma)\, u)
\end{equation}
and the linear relationship of Equation~\ref{eqn:eje-slope} becomes:
$$
\ln \frac{dJ}{du} = \ln(\alpha) + (1-\gamma) u
$$
Direct integration of Equation~\ref{eqn:djdu} gives:
\begin{equation}
  J(u) \equiv \int^\infty_u \; \frac{dJ}{du} \; du= 
  \frac{\alpha}{\gamma - 1} \exp((1-\gamma)\, u) = \frac{1}{\gamma-1} \frac{dJ}{du}
\end{equation}
which we could also obtain by substituting Equation~\ref{eqn:djdu-edjde} into Equation~\ref{eqn:je}.

\section{Shower Energy generation}

The power-law is a convenient model for both the analytic calculations
that follow and for toy Monte Carlo shower generation which is used to
validate our results.  The power law is only approximate, or,
equivalently, the parameters of the power law vary with shower energy.
However, our results below include an estimate for a minimum
detectable shower energy, and we expect that only a narrow energy
region above this minimum will contribute significantly to our
experimental rate.  As long as the parameters for the power law are
taken for the energy range appropriate for our experiment, we expect
that our results will be accurate.

We simulate showers by drawing a random variable $u$, the
dimensionless log-energy of the shower, following the distribution
$dJ/du$.  This is equivalent to drawing a primary energy $E$ according
to the power-law distribution $dJ/dE$, but is less susceptible to
round-off errors.  To avoid the divergence at $E=0$, we set a minimum
shower energy $E_a$ and define:
$$a \equiv \ln (E_a / \epsilon)$$
The cumulative distribution function is:
$$P(u) = \frac{J(a) - J(u)}{J(a)}$$
which is the probability that a shower with dimensionless log energy greater than $a$ falls in the interval $[a,u]$.
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
P(u) = 1 - \exp\left( (1-\gamma)\,(u-a) \right)
\end{equation}
which is easily invertible.  Therefore, to generate a random variable $u$, throw $0 \leq x < 1$
and calculate:
\begin{equation}
u = a - \frac{1}{\gamma-1} \ln (1-x)
\end{equation}
After generating $N$ such showers, each should have a weight:
$$w = \frac{1}{N} \, J(a) = \frac{1}{N} \, \frac{\alpha}{\gamma-1} \, \exp\left( (1-\gamma) \, a \, \right)$$
which accounts for the original assumption that the shower has $u > a$.


\section{Incident Cosmic Ray Flux}

Next we calcualte the flux of incident cosmic ray particles when
projected (without any attenuation or scattering) to the surface of
the earth.  We approximate the earth as an infinite plane (appropriate
for our thin atmosphere, as we shall see) and calculate the
flat-surface differential flux as:
$$F = \int  J \cos \theta d\Omega = 2\pi J \int_0^{\pi/2} \cos\theta \, \sin\theta \, d\theta$$
where we integrate only over the upper hemisphere, have included
a factor of $\cos\theta$ for a flat horizontal surface, and used the fact
that $J$ is independent of direction.  Computing the integral we find that:
\begin{equation}
F = C \pi J
\end{equation}
or equivently:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dF}{dE} = C \pi \frac{dJ}{dE}
\end{equation}
where $C=1$ for the flat-surface approximation made here.  The effect
of the Earth's curvature is considered in the Appendix, where it shown
that $C=0.92$.

\section{Local Muon Density from Extensive Air Showers}

The number of muons $N$ produced by a shower of energy $E$ follows a power law:
\begin{equation}
N(E) = \beta \left( \frac{E}{\epsilon} \right)^{\eta}
\end{equation}
Suppose that these $N$ muons have lateral density profile given by:
$$\Phi(r) = \Phi(0) \exp(-r / \lambda)$$
where $\lambda$ is the length scale of the muon lateral profile.  The condition:
$$\int_0^\infty \; \Phi(r) \, 2 \pi r \, dr = N$$
implies that:
\begin{equation}
\Phi(0) = \frac{N(E)}{2 \pi \lambda^2}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\Phi(r) =  \frac{N(E)}{2 \pi \lambda^2} \; \exp(-r / \lambda)
\end{equation}
This is invertible to find an equation for the radius at which the
density falls to $\Phi$:
\begin{equation}
r(\Phi) = -\lambda \ln \left( \frac{2 \pi \lambda^2 \Phi}{N(E)} \right)
\end{equation}

\section{Local Muon Flux from Extensive Air Showers}

A critical consideration for the feasibility of studying extensive air
showers in the lab, using a local network of cell phones, is the rate
of extensive air showers which cause the muon flux in the lab to reach
or exceed a level detectable by the phone network.  A typical phone
has an area times efficiency of
$$A_{\rm eff} = 2 \times 10^{-5}~\rm m^2$$
We define a detectable muon flux $\Phi_D$ as the density at which a
network of $N_{\rm P}$ phones is expected to register at least one hit
on average.  So for $N_{\rm P}=1000$, we have:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\rm D} = \frac{1}{A_{\rm eff}~N_{\rm P} } = 50~{\rm muons} / {\rm m^2}
\end{equation}


We saw in the preceding section that the incident flux of cosmic rays
follows a power law, and the differential intensity is given by
$$ \frac{dJ}{dE} = \frac{\alpha}{\epsilon} \left( \frac{E}{\epsilon} \right)^{-\gamma} $$
and the angle-integrated differential flux:
$$ \frac{dF}{dE} = 0.92 \; \pi \; \frac{dJ}{dE} $$
where we can set 0.92 to one if we want to approximate the earth as an
infinite plane.



which means the area $A_{\rm D}$ over which there is a detectable muon flux is given by:
$$A_{\rm D}(E) = \pi \lambda^2 \; \left( \ln \frac{2 \pi \lambda^2 \Phi_{\rm D}}{N(E)} \right)^2$$
which is only valid ($A_{\rm D} > 0$) when:
$$\Phi(0) = \frac{N(E)}{2\pi\lambda^2} > \Phi_D$$
which sets a minimum energy, as we shall see.  Using the power law, note that:
$$\frac{N(E)}{\Phi_{\rm D} \, 2\pi\lambda^2}
= \frac{\beta}{\Phi_{\rm D} \, 2\pi\lambda^2} \left( \frac{E}{\epsilon}\right)^{\eta} = \left( \frac{E}{\kappa \epsilon}\right)^{\eta}$$
where
\begin{equation}
\kappa = \left( \frac{\Phi_{\rm D} 2 \pi \lambda^2}{\beta}\right)^{1/\eta}
\end{equation}
and so:
\begin{equation}
A_{\rm D}(E) = \pi \lambda^2 \eta^2 \; \left( \ln \frac{E}{\kappa \epsilon} \right)^2
\end{equation}
which is only valid for $E > \kappa \epsilon$ (which you can verify is equivalent to requiring that $\Phi(0) > \Phi_{\rm D}$).

We are interested in the quantity:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dN}{dE dt} = \frac{dF}{dE} \; A_{\rm D}(E) = (0.92) \pi \frac{dJ}{dE} A_{\rm D}(E)  
\end{equation}
Plugging in the results below we obtain:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dN}{dE \, dt} = (0.92) \frac{\alpha}{\epsilon} \, (\pi \lambda \eta)^2 \, \left( \frac{E}{\epsilon}\right)^{-\gamma}
\left( \ln \frac{E}{\kappa \epsilon}\right)^2
\end{equation}
which we can integrate from $E=\kappa \epsilon$ to $\infty$ and obtain our main result:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dN}{dt} = (0.92) \alpha \, (\pi \lambda \eta)^2 \kappa^{1-\gamma} \,
  \frac{2}{(\gamma - 1)^3}
\end{equation}


\section{Projections}
\begin{itemize}

\item The cosmic ray background is 170 muons per second per m$^2$.  If
  phones integrate 1 s, that amounts to 170 muons per m$^2$ of muon
  flux, which any signal must overcome. 

\item We could try to directly reduce this background, e.g. by siting
  the pilot study underground.

\item We could improve the timing resolution of the phones.  At 1/10 s
  the background density becomes 17 muons per m$^2$, and at 1/100 s, 1.7
  muons per m$^2$. 
  
\item If our tagger is set to trigger at muon flux higher than the background flux, we can reliably get a signal that exceeds background.  But our sample size is limited by the rate at which EAS cause showers.  For example (with $\lambda=200 m)$\\[4pt]
\begin{tabular}{ll}
muons / m$^2$ & showers per year \\
340 & 0.02 \\
100 & 0.4 \\
50  & 2.0 \\
20  & 14 \\
10  & 63 \\
5   & 282 \\
\end{tabular}

\item We could add sites or years to improve these statistics, but
  rejecting background seems more promising to me.

\item Almost certainly we can get a factor of 1/10 reduction, with
  limited additional deadtime, by using an external trigger.  Perhaps
  even we can do this on the phone, with a better designed pipeline.

\item Getting a factor of 1/100th might be possible with external
  trigger, but deadtime is likely becoming significant (50\% assuming
  readout is ~ 10 ms).

\item 1/10 time resolution plus overburden 1/10 gets us to 1.7 muons / m$^2$, and 282 events per year well exceeding this...

\end{itemize}

\section{Effect of Earth's Curvature}

Our earth has atmosphere of height $h$ and radius $R$.  An incident
cosmic ray primary has some impact parameter $b$, which is the same
whethere measured at TOA or on the ground.  At TOA we have incident
angle $\theta_h$ and:
$$\sin \theta_h = \frac{b}{R+h}$$
while at ground we have incident angle $\theta_g$ and:
$$\sin \theta_g = \frac{b}{R}$$
so that:
\begin{equation}
(R+h) \sin \theta_h = R \sin \theta_g
\end{equation}
The Jacobian:
$$\frac{d\Omega_h}{d\Omega_g} = \left( \frac{R}{R+h} \right)^2 \frac{\cos \theta_g}{\sqrt{1 - \left( \frac{R}{R+h} \right)^2 \sin^2\theta_g}}$$
and the resulting incident flat-surface differential flux (now accounting for earth's shape) is:
$$F = 0.92 \, \pi \, J $$
for TOA at $h=100~\rm km$ and earth radius $R=6371~\rm km$.
